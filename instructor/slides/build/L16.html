    Decision trees: fold back the EMV

    flowchart LR D{"Build in-house?<br>or Buy?"} -->|Build, P=0.6 good| G["+2.0M"] D -->|Build, P=0.4 bad| B["-0.8M"] D -->|Buy| BU["0.4M certain"]
  • In-house EMV = 0.6 × 2.0 + 0.4 × (−0.8) = +0.88M vs buy = 0.4M → build wins on average
  • Fold-back answers "if we are risk-neutral" — the debate is whether you are
    • Speaker notes
    The risk-neutrality caveat is the teaching package's key discussion — a cash-poor sponsor may rationally buy. The tree is the deck's one non-Mermaid visual spot... actually keep it Mermaid for consistency. 5 min.

    Monte Carlo: what the machine actually does

  • Sample every activity duration from its distribution (L10's O/M/P)
  • Run the network → one simulated finish date
  • Repeat 10,000× → a distribution of finish dates
    • flowchart LR S["Sample<br>durations"] --> N["Run network<br>once"] N --> R["Record<br>finish date"] R -->|x10,000| H["Histogram"]
  • The critical path can change between runs — L11's near-critical path (TF=1) becomes critical in many trials
    • Speaker notes
    This connects directly to L11's F-activity subtlety — students now see why float ranks can flip. Lab 11's script does exactly this with a fixed seed. 5 min.

    • Speaker notes
    Every number is checker-verified. The conditional-vs-unconditional distinction is the statistics teaching point of the year for this cohort — CS-22's whole argument. 6 min.

    Summary & exit ticket

  • Decision trees fold back EMVs — under an explicit risk-neutrality assumption
  • Monte Carlo = sample → run → repeat; distributions, not dates
  • Report P70, not the median; conditional ≠ unconditional means
